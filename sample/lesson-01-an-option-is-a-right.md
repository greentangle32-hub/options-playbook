# LESSON OB-01 — An Option Is a Right

- **Part:** I — The Basics
- **Prerequisites:** None — this is the first lesson
- **Next lesson:** OB-02 — Call = Up. Put = Down. (the two directions a right can point)
- **Visual page:** Options Playbook, "An Option Is a Right"
- **Rule for every example in this pack:** hypothetical only. Never present an example as a trade suggestion.

## Concepts

### OB-01-01 — A right, not an obligation
An option contract gives the buyer the right — never the duty — to buy or sell 100 shares at a set price by a set date. The buyer chooses whether to use it. Nobody can force the buyer's hand.

### OB-01-02 — The premium buys the right
The premium is the price of the right itself, paid once, up front. It is not a deposit and not part-payment toward the shares. It is the full price of the choice.

### OB-01-03 — The seller carries the duty
The seller collects the premium and takes the other side: if the buyer uses the right, the seller must perform. The seller is paid precisely because the choice is not theirs.

### OB-01-04 — Walking away
If using the right makes no sense at expiry, the buyer simply lets it lapse. The loss is the premium — known on day one, and never a cent more.

### OB-01-05 — The key, not the deed
An option is like a key that can open a door before a deadline, at a price agreed today. Owning the key is not owning the house. It is owning the *choice* to enter.

## Worked examples (hypothetical)

### Example A — the house key (the picture before the market)
A house is listed at $300,000. You pay the owner $3,000 for a signed agreement: any time in the next 60 days, you may buy it at $300,000 — and you don't have to.
- If prices jump to $340,000, your agreement holds $40,000 of advantage (before the $3,000 you paid for it): buy at the agreed price, or sell the agreement itself.
- If prices fall to $270,000, you walk away. The $3,000 is gone — and that is the entire loss. You never owed the difference.

### Example B — the same shape in the market
XYZ trades at $50. A 50-strike call costs $2.00 — **$200 per contract** (× 100 shares, see OB-03).
- XYZ runs to $58 by expiry: the right lets you buy at $50 what is worth $58. The right is worth $8 a share = $800; your profit is $800 − $200 = **$600**. (A shareholder gained $800 — you gained the same move, minus the price of the right.)
- XYZ falls to $44 instead: the right is worthless, you walk away, and your loss is exactly **$200** — while a shareholder is down $600 on 100 shares.

## Common misconceptions

1. **"Buying an option means owning the stock."** You own a right *about* the stock — no shares, no dividends, no votes — until and unless you exercise it.
2. **"If the option expires unused, I might owe more."** The buyer's total possible loss is the premium paid. Nothing can be billed afterward.
3. **"The premium goes toward the purchase price."** The premium pays for the right itself. Exercising means paying the strike *in addition* — the premium is spent either way.

## Quiz bank (scenario style — ask one at a time; explain each answer in one line)

**Q1 · OB-01-01.** You hold a call on XYZ (strike $50) that cost you $2. The stock sits at $46 at expiry. What must you do?
- a) Buy 100 shares at $50 — you signed a contract *(misconception: an option is a duty for both sides)*
- b) Let it lapse; you lose the $200 premium and nothing more ✔
- c) Pay the seller the $4 difference *(misconception: the buyer owes the gap, like a margin balance)*
- d) It rolls over automatically at no cost *(misconception: expiry quietly extends contracts)*
**Why:** The buyer holds a right, not a duty. At $46 the right is worthless, and walking away costs exactly the premium.

**Q2 · OB-01-02.** What does the $2 premium actually purchase?
- a) 100 shares, on layaway *(misconception: premium as part-payment)*
- b) The right to buy at $50 by the deadline — the choice itself ✔
- c) Insurance that refunds losses on shares you own *(misconception: every option is a hedge)*
- d) A loan from the seller *(misconception: premium as borrowed money)*
**Why:** The premium buys the choice. The shares, if ever bought, cost the strike price on top.

**Q3 · OB-01-03.** You sold a call and collected $200. The buyer lets it expire worthless. What do you owe?
- a) The $200 back *(misconception: premium as a refundable deposit)*
- b) Nothing — you keep the $200 ✔
- c) 100 shares, delivered anyway *(misconception: the seller performs regardless)*
**Why:** The seller's duty only activates if the buyer exercises. The right lapsed, so the premium is simply kept.

**Q4 · OB-01-04.** A friend says: "I bought a call, so now I own a piece of the company — dividends and all." The accurate correction?
- a) True, once the option is in profit *(misconception: profit converts a right into ownership)*
- b) A call is a right about the stock, not the stock — no dividends or votes until exercise ✔
- c) True, but only on the day of expiry *(misconception: ownership switches on at the deadline)*
**Why:** Ownership begins at exercise, not at the purchase of the right.

**Q5 · OB-01-02.** You pay $200 for a call. The stock never moves at all. At expiry you are down…
- a) $0 — an unused option refunds *(misconception: unused means refundable)*
- b) $200 — the premium, in full ✔
- c) It depends on the broker's policy *(misconception: outcomes are discretionary)*
**Why:** A right you never use still cost what it cost. The premium bought time and choice; both were delivered.

**Spaced repetition rule:** any question missed resurfaces on day 3 and again on day 7, re-asked with different numbers. Two clean passes in a row retire the question.
